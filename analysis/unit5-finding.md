# Unit 5 Finding: Two Kinds of Problem Flights Need Two Different Fixes

**To:** Leadership team
**From:** Trust and Data team
**Re:** Flights with high no-show rates, and flights that have never sold a seat

Our earlier reports looked only at flights that had bookings. Once we included every flight on the platform, a second group showed up. Of our 15 flights, 13 have bookings and 2 (VN214 and VN215) have never had a single one. Those two were missing from every report you have seen so far, because the reports started from bookings and a flight with no bookings had nothing to show. Across the platform, about 6 in every 100 bookings end with the passenger not turning up.

The two groups have different problems and need different responses. Where people book but don't turn up, the fix is about passengers. Where nobody books at all, the fix is about how the flight is set up.

**Flights that sell but where passengers often don't show up: VN202 (and VN203, VN204).** VN202 is the clear case. It has 16 bookings and 3 no-shows, which is about three times the platform rate, and enough bookings that it is unlikely to be chance. The flight itself is fine: people want it and book it. VN211, another Japan-based flight, has exactly the same number of bookings and only a third of the no-shows. The problem is what happens between booking and boarding. Airports don't explain it either: flights serving the same airports as VN202 have few or no no-shows. One thing all three flagged flights share is that they run 320-seat aircraft, the largest among their peers. That is worth a look, but it is not proven: another 320-seat flight, VN205, has no no-shows at all. **We recommend reviewing VN202's booking and reminder process**: whether passengers get check-in reminders, how far ahead they book, and whether the fare rules make it too easy to skip a flight without cost. If the rate stays high after that, adjust the fare or overbooking policy on that flight. VN203 has already been taken out of service, so no action is needed beyond noting it. VN204 has only 6 bookings, so a single no-show makes its rate look high. **Keep watching it, but don't act yet.**

**Flights that have never sold a seat: VN214 and VN215.** Here the issue is not passenger behaviour. Nobody has booked at all. Both flights are listed as active and have been on the schedule since early 2022, so they are not new and they have not been retired. What stands out is that **neither flight has any route set up**: no departure or arrival airport is linked to either of them. A flight with no airports most likely can't be found or booked by customers, which would explain why nobody has booked it. We can't prove that from the data alone, and it needs checking with the team that sets up flights. **We recommend fixing the route setup for VN214 and VN215 first**, then watching bookings for a set period, for example 60 days. If they still don't sell once customers can find them, that is a demand problem, and the choice is between a price test and withdrawing the flight. If they were never meant to fly, mark them inactive so they stop counting as part of our network. While doing this, the team should also look at VN213, which has bookings but no route on record either.

**Going forward,** reports on flight performance should start from the full list of flights, not from bookings. That way a flight that has stopped selling shows up as a zero instead of disappearing from the report.
